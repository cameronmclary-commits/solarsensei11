Your tariff can make or break your solar return

Time-of-use (TOU) tariffs charge different rates at different times of day. This can significantly affect the value of your solar generation, the attractiveness of battery storage, and whether changing plans may be more valuable than installing equipment.

How TOU tariffs affect solar value

The timing mismatch

Solar generates through the middle of the day — often during shoulder or off-peak pricing — while many households consume most energy in the morning and, especially, the evening peak:

How TOU tariffs affect battery value

1

Time-shift solar

Store excess daytime solar and discharge in the evening peak, avoiding expensive grid imports.

2

Charge off-peak

Where your plan allows, charge from the grid overnight and discharge during peak — capturing the rate difference.

3

Manage load

Cover high-cost periods from storage and potentially reduce demand charges on demand tariffs.

So — do batteries stack up on TOU?

More attractive when…

  • Peak rates are significantly higher than off-peak (e.g. 35¢ vs 12¢)
  • Feed-in tariffs are low (5–10¢), making self-consumption valuable
  • Evening consumption is high, creating peak-import displacement opportunities
  • Solar generation exceeds daytime use, leaving surplus to store

Less attractive when…

  • Rate differences are small (e.g. 28¢ vs 22¢) — limited arbitrage
  • Feed-in tariffs are generous (15–20¢) — exporting competes with storing
  • Evening consumption is low — little expensive demand to displace
  • You're on a flat-rate tariff — no time-based pricing to exploit

Should you change your electricity plan?

Many homeowners remain on default or legacy tariffs without realising better-fitting options exist. Before installing solar or batteries, consider:

  • Current structure — are you on flat-rate or time-of-use?
  • Alternatives — do other plans price your usage pattern better?
  • Feed-in comparisons — do some retailers pay more for exports?
  • Peak alignment — do peak windows match your high-consumption hours?

Changing plans may sometimes deliver more immediate savings than installing additional equipment — which is why Solar Sensei shows the two effects separately.

Frequently asked questions

What are typical peak, shoulder and off-peak times?

Peak is typically late afternoon to evening (e.g. 4pm–9pm weekdays), shoulder covers daytime and early evening outside peak, and off-peak is usually overnight (e.g. 10pm–7am). Exact windows vary by retailer, state and specific tariff.

Is solar still worth it on a time-of-use tariff?

Generally yes, but timing matters. Solar generation often lands in shoulder or off-peak pricing periods while household consumption peaks in expensive evening periods. This makes self-consumption timing, and potentially battery storage, more important.

Do batteries work with time-of-use tariffs?

They can work well: time-shift solar into the evening peak, charge off-peak where your plan allows, and manage load to cover high-cost periods. They are most attractive when peak rates far exceed off-peak rates and evening consumption is high.

Should I change plans before installing solar?

It is worth comparing first. Check your current structure, alternatives that price your usage pattern better, feed-in comparisons and peak alignment — a plan change can sometimes deliver more immediate savings than new equipment.

Does time-of-use pricing change feed-in tariff value?

It changes the trade-off: every kWh you self-consume saves the import rate for that period rather than earning the feed-in rate, so low feed-in tariffs combined with high peak import rates make self-consumption — and storage — more valuable.
